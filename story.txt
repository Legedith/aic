I Took a $20 ChatGPT Plan Into a $180,000 Robotics Competition. I Finished 8th.
27 Oct 2026 · @legedith
In 2026 I finished 8th in the world at a robotics challenge run by Intrinsic, an Alphabet company. I did it with no robotics degree, a four-year gap since I last touched ROS, a 2018 laptop with a GTX 1660, and a $20 ChatGPT plan.
I couldn't iterate fast, so I stopped trying to solve the problem myself. I built a system that could iterate without me, and then I managed the system. This is the story of how I built that system and used it to return to robotics.
A quick map for the road ahead: a three-month simulation qualifier, a six-week phase in Intrinsic's Flowstate where I got a vision model and a trick with exported processes, and a one-month finale on a real UR5e robot at Intrinsic's headquarters, controlled remotely from my desk.
The challenge, in plain words
The AI for Industry Challenge was run by Intrinsic and Open Robotics, with Foxconn, Google DeepMind, NVIDIA and Universal Robots as partners. The task sounds almost silly: plug a network cable into a board. One end of the cable has an SFP plug, the other an SC plug. They look nothing alike, and neither do their ports. You write the policy, which is just the logic that decides how the robot behaves, and the robot has to find the right plug, pick it up, and push it home.
The scale surprised everyone, including the organizers. According to Intrinsic's own recap, about 5,000 developers from 115 countries took part, and The Robot Report counts about 1,600 teams. Only 14% of participants worked in robotics. The prize pool was $180,000, and the field included labs, companies and professors.
The challenge ran in three phases, each narrowing the field:
1.  Qualification (March 2 to mid-May 2026). Everything in simulation, using Gazebo. The robot starts already holding the plug, the board position is randomized, and you are told which port to use. Around 160 teams made the leaderboard, and the top 30 advanced.
2.  Phase 1 (mid-May to July). The top 30 teams got Intrinsic Flowstate and the Intrinsic Vision Model. Now the robot has to find the cable, grasp it, pull it out and insert both ends. Still simulated, but roughly five times harder.
3.  Phase 2 (about a month, ending in September). The top 10 teams deployed remotely onto a real UR5e workcell at Intrinsic HQ in California, with a Robotiq gripper, a force-torque sensor and three wrist cameras. Only two teams reached 100% success in the final stage.
Qualification: build the team, not the policy
Let me be honest about where I started. I have no robotics degree, no controls course, nothing formal. I had worked in robotics years earlier, back when ROS 1 Noetic had just come out and ROS 2 was still unstable. Then I left the field. When I read the challenge, I was overwhelmed.
The other side of the leaderboard looked different. There were teams from Ivy League universities, robotics labs and big corporations, many led by professors and PhDs. I had a personal ChatGPT plan and a 2018 laptop with an Intel i7 and a GTX 1660. In Gazebo, the simulator, I ran at a real-time factor of 0.15 to 0.2, meaning the simulation moved at roughly a fifth of real speed, in the best case. Every experiment was painfully slow.
So I changed the question. Instead of asking how to solve the challenge, I asked how to build the system that would solve it for me.
I wiped a partition, installed a fresh Ubuntu, and dedicated it to the challenge. Then I used Codex inside VS Code to install and verify everything itself. When it hit a bug, I didn't fix the bug. I asked a different question: why couldn't Codex figure this out, and what could I give it so that next time it would? That meant memory files, fetched documentation, guides and custom workflows, including one dedicated to testing any policy, using the organizers' sample policies as checks. By the end I hadn't typed a single install command by hand.
The harness grew from there. Codex could build a policy, package it, test it and hand me the upload. It could run for days while I was away, as long as I had power and internet. In May, long-running tasks were poorly supported, and context compaction caused constant trouble, so a lot of my effort went into helping Codex survive its own long memory. And because a $20 plan has limits, I learned to spend them carefully: a cheaper model to gather context and do research, a stronger model for the hard thinking, and an orchestrator coordinating sub-agents so that cached context got reused instead of rebuilt.
Once I could describe the challenge to it as a human would, I could ask it open questions: what are my options, and what will go right or wrong with each? Many of its suggestions, like YOLO-based detection, HIL-SERL, HG-DAgger and classical OpenCV features, showed up later in other participants' write-ups. I settled on a very small YOLO model, trained by Codex on data collected from the organizers' reference policy, with me supervising.
I also played the scoring rules differently. Everyone else, I assume, was chasing the top score. I was trying to stay on the leaderboard: do enough insertions to count, then maximize what I was best at, whether that was trajectory quality, proximity or insertion. My results arrived late, so I made far fewer of my daily submissions than most teams. I finished 19th with a score of about 220, comfortably inside the top 30 and well behind the early leaders, who were near 293.
Phase 1: a visual tool, a hidden door, and a very good vision model
Phase 1 raised the difficulty in every direction. The robot no longer started with the plug in hand. It had to find the cable, grasp it, pull it out, insert one end, then do the other. We had about six weeks instead of three months, and a brand new tool to learn.
That tool was Intrinsic Flowstate, a web-based environment where you design a workcell and author robot behavior as a flowchart-like behavior tree. It's genuinely intuitive for humans. It was also a problem for me, because my whole advantage was an AI agent that worked in text and code, and Flowstate was visual-first. Flowstate also had its off days: it sometimes failed for me because of capacity problems, which is understandable for a young platform suddenly used by a crowd.
The hidden door
While clicking around I noticed that any flow you build, which Flowstate calls a process, can be exported. And an exported process can be imported back. I tried it, and it worked.
If you can teach an agent to read, modify and rebuild those exported files, you have a way to iterate on the robot's behavior without touching the UI. Around then, Intrinsic released its command-line tool, inctl, part of the public SDK, which could list solutions, inspect processes and install them. I built a harness around it. From there my loop was simple: run the process, watch what happened, discuss the results with Codex, let it write the change, install it into Flowstate, and run again. As far as I know, I was the only team working this way.
This paid off in an unexpected way. Flowstate sometimes wouldn't save my edits, which turned out to be a platform issue. Because I always had a local copy of every process, I could spin up a fresh VM and reinstall, or debug offline. Others reportedly lost work near the deadline.
The model that made the policy unnecessary
Phase 1 also gave us the Intrinsic Vision Model (IVM). It's based on Intrinsic's 3PT paper, the 3D-Object Perception Transformer, which was a CVPR 2026 highlight (top 3% of submissions). It estimates 6D poses from ordinary RGB cameras, with no depth sensor, by fusing views from multiple angles. Intrinsic's recap says all of the top 10 finalists used it.
Its raw accuracy beat everything I'd built for qualification, with no extra work. Tested from the robot's home pose it already gave me 3 to 5 millimeters of accuracy and repeatability. My trained YOLO model was suddenly obsolete.
The scan lab
Three to five millimeters isn't enough to push a connector into a port, so I went after the error systematically. I read the 3PT paper, then told Codex we would imitate their experiments on our own components: find the camera distances, approach angles and combinations of views with the least variance. That became a reusable lab. It tests viewpoints and how many observations are needed, and it tells me how to weight and fuse them.
Then I added geometry. The taskboard has five NIC cards sitting parallel on rails, with known spacing, and the SC ports sit in two known rows. If you average the orientations of parallel components, the angular error drops. If you know everything sits on the board, the height error drops. The NIC cards are the biggest objects and the most reliably detected, so they can pin down the rest. Each scan updated a belief the robot held about the board, so more observations kept tightening it. Against the simulator's ground truth, the fused estimate was within a millimeter, often by a good margin, and for practical purposes indistinguishable from the real thing.
One of the nicest things in my notes is how a wrong assumption cost more than a weak model. In Phase 2, I found that my SC ports were off by about 18 mm after fusion, even though the raw detections were accurate. The culprit was a geometric prior from Phase 1 expressed in the wrong coordinate frame. Fixing the frame solved it. More images wouldn't have.
The cable, meanwhile, was the sporadic part. Flexible cables are hard to simulate, and in Phase 1 a lot of my custom skills existed only to manage how the simulated cable misbehaved. I started the run from a high vantage point: one part of the board was always visible, and from up high even low-confidence IVM detections were enough to locate the board roughly. Then I moved closer and scanned properly.
Phase 2: real hardware, and a deliberately dumb policy
Phase 2 gave each team a limited number of slots on a real robot, about twelve. I only managed to use seven or eight of them, partly through some bad scheduling on my side. Hardware runs didn't count toward the final score. They were for learning.
The good news was that my whole system carried over. The same scan lab I'd used in simulation found good camera poses on the real arm. The same Codex loop edited and installed processes. Every Wednesday the cameras were recalibrated, and each time IVM's accuracy jumped, which showed how completely my solution depended on good perception.
The big change was that I could delete things. In Phase 1 I'd written custom motion skills to cope with the simulated robot, and motions that were harmless there could hurt real equipment. For Phase 2 I dropped them and used Flowstate's native motion skills, which gave more predictable, more constrained movement. The cable also behaved more consistently on hardware than in simulation, so I could plan around its expected movement instead of fighting it.
Insertion without looking
Once the robot had the plug within a few millimeters of the port, I stopped using cameras altogether. The descent, the search and the push were all driven by contact. The paper behind the vision model describes something similar for its own insertion tests, so I was in good company.
The controller combined Cartesian admittance control, which yields when it feels force, with joint-space impedance control, which keeps the arm stable. For the SFP plug, the robot descended slowly until it touched the port face, then searched with a smooth sweep across the opening. I started with a Lissajous-style path, then moved to spirals and raster-like sweeps that covered the opening better. If it felt force pushing from one side, it twisted or shifted the other way.
The SC plug mostly didn't need a search, since the vision handoff already landed it very close.
Then I hit a wall. In two runs the plug went almost all the way in but wouldn't click. The Intrinsic team who ran the cell told me something I would never have guessed, since I've never held an SC plug in real life: the SC connector clicks twice. The first click isn't enough, so you have to push further for the second one. That was my eureka moment. The simulator hadn't captured that click either, which Intrinsic later listed as one of the key sim-to-real gaps. My policy now treated the second click as the goal, pulling the plug toward an imagined point inside the port like a rubber band.
A surprise from Intrinsic's own recap: about 90% of the top teams also used a rules-based insertion strategy, mostly spiral searches, pattern matching and alignment corrections. A dumb policy on top of an excellent vision model isn't a hack. It turned out to be what most of the winners did too.
When doing something isn't progress
My notes from the final weeks are full of bugs that only real hardware could show. In one SC failure, the plug was captured and pushed 8 to 9 mm into the port, and then a retreat of a fraction of a millimeter triggered a recovery move that backed it out. Each attempt wasted about 150 of its 180 seconds undoing its own progress. I rebuilt the logic so that once the plug is captured, the controller only ever pushes forward.
In one SFP attempt, the logs recorded over 300 stalls while the plug sat at the same shallow depth, and the search I'd designed only started about three seconds before the time limit. The robot was constantly busy and getting nowhere. I called it a liveness problem, and fixing it meant looking at progress as measured depth, not just at forces.
Ten insertions, and the limits of certainty
By my third-to-last hardware run, my plugs were reliably arriving at the port even with different board layouts, and I was getting passive insertions that nearly went in. After the double-click fix and a few more rounds, my final hardware run did all ten insertions on the first try. That was five runs with two connectors each. Eight were perfect, with zero gap. The other two were off by about 0.5 mm and 1.5 mm, well inside the three-millimeter margin.
That last hardware session gave me ten successful insertions out of ten, so I entered the final evaluation with high expectations. I didn't get the same 10 out of 10 during official scoring, and finished 8th. That's a reality of robotics: a system can work exceptionally well in testing and still behave differently when evaluated. Calibration, perception and contact dynamics all introduce uncertainty. My leading hypothesis is camera calibration drift. My solution relied heavily on accurate poses from the Intrinsic Vision Model, so a small shift in calibration could affect everything downstream. But that's a hypothesis, not a confirmed explanation.
Two teams reached 100% success in the final stage. Team Flex from the USA won, and TCS-PLGRM from India took second place. Flex used a lateral swinging motion for SC insertion that lets the connector self-align with the port. In hardware, that kind of detail decides things.
I'll remember this experience for life. After years away from robotics, I came back not just to take part but to finish in the top ten in the world, with a shot at the top.
What I took away
1.  When you can't iterate fast, build something that can. I couldn't afford many experiments, so I built a harness of skills, memory files, test workflows, specialized sub-agents and deterministic checks, and let it run for days. I managed the system, and the system managed the project.
2.  Simple can beat clever. When the perception is excellent and you know your environment's randomization, a dumb contact-driven policy can reach 10 out of 10. I felt like an impostor next to teams with elaborate learned systems, but Intrinsic's own recap says most winners did the same.
3.  Know what your evidence proves. My notes separate built, installed, loaded, run and observed on hardware, because each is a different claim. A fix that passes tests isn't a fix until the robot agrees.
4.  Sim is a rehearsal, not the show. The click, the cable and the contact physics all changed on hardware. Removing simulator workarounds made my system better.
5.  Domain expertise matters less than it used to, but management matters more. I did all of this on a personal plan. Imagine what a team with real resources could do. You no longer have to be an expert in a field to get close to the top of it, if you're good at directing AI.
A big thank you to Intrinsic and Open Robotics for running a challenge this ambitious, and for letting a laptop from 2018 show up to it.
Appendix: how the Phase 2 system worked
The design rule was to reduce uncertainty step by step and use the sensor that suits each stage: cameras for where things are, force sensing for the last few millimeters.
1.  Scan. The robot goes to a high vantage point, finds the board roughly, then runs a scan sequence designed in the scan lab. Each IVM detection is one noisy observation, not the truth.
2.  Fuse. A constraint solver reconciles the detections using the declared board geometry: parallel NIC cards, rail positions, the SC port rows, and the fact that everything sits on the board. The result is one coherent world model.
3.  Cache and refresh. Board geometry is stored and reused across all five runs. Cable and connector state can change, so just before each pick the robot takes a single IVM look directly above the connector, to check that it hasn't moved or been blocked.
4.  Move with context. Fast through free space. Slow, with fixed validated velocity and acceleration limits, near the board. While carrying a connector, a smooth raised path keeps clearance and avoids swinging the cable.
5.  Hand off to force. Vision brings the plug to within a few millimeters of the port. From there the controller is compliant and force-guided, with no camera feedback: slow descent, contact, a small search for the SFP, and a one-way push for SC, past the second click.
6.  Reset. After each run the robot returns to a standard home pose. Runs two to five repeat only the observations needed to confirm the connector and cable state.
The pieces are configuration, not code tied to one board. A different board layout would need new component models, geometry, scan parameters, grasp frames and insertion parameters, plus validation, but not a new architecture.
The workflow around it
My robot code lived in Flowstate processes, and I treated them as software. A runbook in my repository describes the loop: export the exact saved process, edit it in a controlled way, validate it locally, install it, then fetch it back from the server and compare it with what I sent. The final submitted process had about 500 nodes. The same notes insist on separating seven states, from a local file to a hardware result, because installing a process doesn't run it and loading it doesn't save it.
Caveats
Numbers like sub-millimeter fusion accuracy are measured against simulator ground truth, which doesn't exist on the real robot. The SC stage pushed harder than the SFP stage near its end, so "low force" describes the first contact, not every moment. And everything here describes my own system, not a general recipe for safe robot insertion.
