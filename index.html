ENGINEERING FIELD NOTE / 2026

I Entered a Global Robotics Challenge With a $20 AI Subscription.
Then I Built the Team I Couldn't Afford.

I was one engineer up against research labs and industry teams. My robot needed to locate, carry, and feel its way into tiny connector ports. To compete, I first had to build something else: a team of AI agents that could run the engineering process itself.

00 / THE SECOND CLICK

The robot found the port.
It still couldn't finish.

The clever part was already done. The robot had found the connector, carried a flexible cable across a crowded board, and lined up a tiny plug with the opening. It pushed. The connector looked almost seated.

But almost is an expensive word in robotics.

I was working remotely on a physical robot at Intrinsic's headquarters, trying to solve a problem I had never attempted with my own hands. I had not even handled an SC connector before. Someone familiar with the hardware finally told me what I was missing: the connector could click twice. The first click did not mean the insertion was finished. There was another bit of travel, another mechanical latch, and an entirely different definition of success.

That was the kind of problem I had signed up to solve. Not just moving a robot between coordinates, but getting it to notice what contact was telling it. What would I do if I were holding the cable? Feel an edge, ease off, adjust my wrist, try a different direction, keep pushing only when I could feel that the plug was going in. I even asked friends to reason through the movements with me. It was a surprisingly useful way to think about force sensing.

The strange part was how I got there. I had no robotics degree, no research group, and no company hardware budget. My computer was an old laptop that could barely run the simulator at a useful speed. My AI tooling was a roughly $20-a-month personal subscription. Yet I was competing in a challenge that eventually drew 5,000 developers from 115 countries, including people who worked on robotics for a living.

When the competition ended, I had a final personal hardware session with ten successful insertions and an official eighth-place finish. Those were separate evaluations, and the gap between them still bothers me. But the most important thing I built over those months was not the insertion controller.

It was the engineering team I could not afford to hire.

01 / MARCH TO MAY

The easiest version of the problem was already hard.

I had been away from robotics for roughly four or five years. The last time I worked seriously with it, ROS Noetic was new and ROS 2 was still finding its feet. Since then, most of my engineering had been about AI. I had never studied robotics or control theory formally, and suddenly I was trying to solve a task that combined perception, motion planning, contact mechanics, and flexible cables.

When I saw Intrinsic's AI for Industry Challenge, I wanted to return. Then I looked at the actual task.

The full challenge involved a flexible fiber-optic cable, an SFP module on one end and an SC plug on the other, and a taskboard full of small, adjustable fixtures. The robot had to identify the right parts, grasp a connector, lift it out of its holder, move the dangling cable without catching it on the board, and finally insert each end into its assigned port. The last few millimeters mattered most. A slight misalignment could turn forward motion into a jam, and a connector that looked seated might still be unlatched.

Qualification was the gentle introduction. The robot already held one connector in a known grasp, and the correct destination was specified. But the board and target positions were randomized. I still had to make the policy find the opening, approach it safely, and finish the insertion. Even so, it was a serious controls and perception problem. Intrinsic's public qualification specification describes the small grasp deviations and board randomization that made the task nontrivial.

The field only made the gap feel larger. Research labs, engineers with years of controls experience, university teams, companies, and independent developers were all trying to solve the same thing. I did not have a lab to ask for another experiment, another GPU, or another pair of hands.

My computer did not help. I was working on an aging laptop with an Intel i7 and a GTX 1660-class GPU. On a good day Gazebo managed a real-time factor of about 0.15 to 0.2. One minute of simulated motion could take five to seven minutes to compute. The official toolkit recommended considerably more powerful hardware. I also had a personal ChatGPT subscription, around twenty dollars a month, rather than an enterprise AI budget.

Initially I went down the obvious paths. Basic OpenCV features and shape extraction, small object-detection experiments, learning-based approaches. I explored ideas such as YOLO, HIL-SERL and H-DAgger, looking for something I could make work within my constraints. What I kept running into was that my real bottleneck was not finding another robotics paper.

It was the rate at which I could run an experiment, understand what went wrong, and try something better. I could not buy more compute or a bigger team. I had to change how the engineering itself was done.

02 / THE FIRST BREAKTHROUGH

So I stopped building the robot. I started building the engineer.

I created a fresh Ubuntu environment on a dedicated drive partition and opened the project in VS Code with Codex. But instead of asking Codex to write a policy and hoping for the best, I gave it a different job: build a development environment that it could operate, diagnose, and improve itself.

At first, that meant installing dependencies, running the official examples, checking the simulator, and packaging a policy. When something failed, my instinct changed. I stopped asking, “How do I fix this?” and started asking, “Why was my agent unable to diagnose this, and what could I give it so it could solve the next one?”

That question gradually became the architecture of my entire entry. If a dependency failed, Codex needed a diagnostic procedure. If a simulation stalled, it needed a way to tell a slow run from a frozen one. If an experiment produced a promising result, it needed to preserve the code, conditions, and evidence, not just tell me it had succeeded.

By the time it matured, the harness was far beyond a set of prompts. It had organized knowledge files, reusable skills, test and packaging workflows, deterministic checks, and judgment-based reviews of logs and behavior. Different agents had distinct responsibilities and their own working contexts. A stronger reasoning model could orchestrate; cheaper models could retrieve documentation or assemble evidence; another focused model could work on a tightly scoped engineering problem.

One agent might investigate a failing build. Another would compare experiment logs. Another could draft the fix. If a result survived the checks, it was added to the project's working knowledge. If it failed, the failure itself became part of the record. The point was not to spawn agents indiscriminately. Every additional agent needed a job, a context boundary, and a way for me to tell whether its output was useful.

One of my favorite patterns was to have an agent assemble a compact problem packet. Instead of feeding an expensive reasoning model the entire project history, it would get the exact error, relevant source, constraints, prior attempts and acceptance criteria. If it needed something else, it requested targeted research. This was as much about conserving a limited model budget as it was about correctness.

I kept durable notes so a session could end without erasing months of learning. The agent was expected to run experiments, inspect the results, notice repeated failure signatures, retry when it made sense, and preserve the conditions under which something had actually worked. Some iterations continued over long stretches with little intervention, provided the laptop and internet held up.

That did not make me unnecessary. My job shifted upward. I decided which experiment was worth running, challenged weak conclusions, and made sure an agent could tell the difference between “the code compiled,” “the simulator ran,” and “the robot actually succeeded.” The more reliable the harness became, the more of the mechanical work it could take over.

While that system was growing, I still needed a qualification policy. I eventually trained a small YOLO-based detector from data collected using the organizer's public example infrastructure. I deliberately stopped chasing a perfect insertion policy. Qualification rewarded several aspects of performance, so I focused on earning enough points reliably to advance. With my simulator running five to seven times slower than real time and official submissions limited, an elegant experiment I could not finish was worth less than a simple one I could actually validate.

By my recollection, I finished qualification around 19th, at roughly 220 points. It was enough to advance. The exact points figure is from memory, but the feeling was definite: the tiny lab I had built on my laptop had survived its first real test. Then, just as my workflow started to feel reliable, the next phase changed the rules of the game.

03 / PHASE 1

A better platform. A completely new bottleneck.

Phase 1 changed the problem dramatically. We no longer started with a connector already in the gripper. The robot had to locate the source cable, determine how to grasp it, extract an end, carry it across the workspace, and insert both ends in sequence. The cable itself was now part of the manipulation problem, not merely something trailing from a held connector. And we had about six weeks.

We also gained access to Intrinsic Flowstate, a development environment that combines a graphical process editor with robot skills, simulation, and programmable capabilities. Intrinsic publicly describes building behavior trees from skills and control flows, extending them through Python or C++, and testing in simulation before using hardware. We were also given access to the Intrinsic Vision Model, or IVM.

Flowstate was powerful, but it was visual-first. My qualification harness had learned to operate a conventional source-code project. A visual process editor did not fit that workflow naturally. I could build things manually in the UI, but that would put me back in the position of being the bottleneck.

Eventually I found a way to keep the process itself in my normal engineering loop. Instead of treating the graphical editor as the only place where work existed, I used locally versioned process artifacts and programmatic tooling alongside Flowstate's visual interface. The publicly available Intrinsic SDK provided a useful development foundation. I could compare versions, validate changes, preserve known-good work, and recover quickly when an editing session or remote environment did not cooperate.

That was my second harness. The first had taught Codex to operate a robotics codebase. The second let the agent help me manage a visual robotics workflow. My human loop became: watch a run, inspect the evidence, decide what was wrong, describe the next experiment, then review the generated change before testing again. It brought the pace of AI-assisted iteration back to a problem that initially seemed to require constant manual editing.

The need for that workflow became obvious as soon as I started dealing with the cable. Simulation could send it into large, awkward arcs. The robot might reach the correct grasp, only for the cable to swing into the next motion's path. I built custom motions to twist, lift, and route the free cable through the environment. Those were not the behaviors I originally wanted to spend six weeks writing, but without them the supposedly simple pick-and-insert task could not even reach its final stage.

Later, physical cable behavior would turn out to be easier to manage in several respects. At the time, though, every simulation-specific workaround made the workflow more important: the less time I spent rebuilding a process, the more time I had left to actually study the robot.

Then Flowstate gave me access to something that made much of my qualification perception work feel obsolete: the Intrinsic Vision Model. The next question was no longer how to make my little detector more capable. It was how far I could push the accuracy of an already excellent pose estimator.

04 / PERCEPTION FIRST

The moment I realized I could throw away my clever policy.

The Intrinsic Vision Model is designed to detect objects and estimate their 3D positions and orientations from ordinary RGB camera images. Its publicly documented research basis, 3PT, the 3D-Object Perception Transformer, uses 3D model prompts, multiview geometry and refinement to estimate six-degree-of-freedom poses. The paper received a CVPR 2026 Highlight recognition.

I had spent considerable effort squeezing useful estimates out of a tiny detector on my machine. Then I tried IVM from the robot's home viewpoint. With very little additional work, I was seeing repeatable results around 3 to 5 mm for the components I cared about. That was already competitive with what I had struggled to build.

Instead of immediately writing a more sophisticated motion policy, I went and read the 3PT work. My question became: What camera viewpoints make this estimator most reliable on this specific taskboard, and what happens when I combine observations?

I asked Codex to help build a scan laboratory. It tested candidate viewpoints, camera distances, approach angles, repeated observations, and fusion strategies. Some results were striking. In simulation, with known taskboard geometry and an evaluation reference, parts of the constrained reconstruction reached submillimeter error. I want to be precise about that: those numbers were geometry-constrained simulation results, not a promise that the raw camera model achieved submillimeter hardware accuracy in every scene.

The next improvement came from treating the taskboard as a system, not a bag of unrelated detections.

GEOMETRIC INTUITION / 02

Five noisy observations. One coherent board.

Structural constraint illustration, not a screenshot, benchmark plot, or literal reconstruction of the challenge board.

Five NIC cards sat in a known structural relationship on parallel rails. Their orientations had to be mutually consistent. Other fixtures had known relationships to those rails. Components sat on known planes and inside known regions of the board. Once I could estimate the larger, easier-to-see objects reliably, I could use those relationships to constrain the others.

This did two things. Averaging consistent observations could reduce noise. More importantly, impossible independent estimates could be rejected or corrected before they reached the motion planner. A card rotated wildly relative to its neighbors was less plausible than a small error in a camera observation. The whole board became one shared geometric hypothesis.

I also split observation into two scales. Initially, with an unknown board position, I moved to a high viewpoint and looked for a visible feature, often a NIC. I did not need an accurate board pose yet, only enough information to plan a safe closer scan. Each subsequent viewpoint improved the estimate. Once the static board was registered, I cached it and refreshed only the changing cable or connector state when needed.

This was the real breakthrough. Instead of trying to make a learned insertion policy robust to every centimeter of pose error, I was pushing uncertainty out of the manipulation stage. If I could reliably approach within a millimeter or two of the actual opening, the final contact behavior could be much simpler.

There was an important trap in that approach, which I discovered through testing: a geometric constraint is only as good as its coordinate frame. In one diagnostic, the raw SC detections looked sensible but an incorrect reference-frame assumption pulled the fused result away from them. That taught me not to confuse a very tidy-looking world model with a correct one. I learned to inspect raw estimates and constrained output separately.

I no longer needed the qualification policy to carry the whole challenge. I could use strong perception, known geometry, deliberate motion, and a small contact-aware state machine.

05 / THE LAST MILLIMETERS

Vision got me close. Contact got me the rest of the way.

When I say my final insertion was “visionless,” that needs a qualifier. I was not running a blind robot. Perception had already established the target, grasp, and path. The insertion controller simply stopped asking the camera for new images once it reached the last few millimeters. It worked with measured motion and force/torque information instead.

Before I tried to make a robot do this, I asked a much simpler question: what would my own hand do? Suppose I know approximately where a plug belongs, but I cannot rely on the exact visual position anymore. I move toward the opening. I feel contact. If the plug catches an edge, I do not immediately apply more force. I shift slightly, change the angle of my wrist, and try again. When the resistance changes and the plug begins to advance, I remember that progress. I do not throw it away just because my hand feels another little bump.

I asked friends to imagine and act through the movement with me. The useful part was not that we could perfectly model a connector by waving our hands around. It was discovering the sequence of questions a person answers unconsciously: Am I touching the face? Is the opening above or to one side? Have I made axial progress? Am I wedged? Has the latch actually engaged? Those questions mapped surprisingly well to a contact-state controller.

In other words, the robot did not need a new picture of the port every few milliseconds. It needed a way to tell the difference between touching the wrong place and moving through the right place. Vision handled the first part of the task; measured depth, force, torque, and compliance handled the rest.

For the SFP module, the handoff was on the order of a few millimeters from the mouth. For the SC plug, it could be closer still. A controller would descend gently, watch whether the connector actually advanced, and make small corrections if contact stopped that progress. Because SFP and SC geometry is different, the corrections could not be identical.

I explored several lateral search shapes: a two-frequency Lissajous path, an expanding spiral, and a serpentine or raster pattern that swept back and forth across an area. A search path needs to cover a plausible opening efficiently without asking the connector to skate across the face under uncontrolled load. Some of the hardest failures were not dramatic collisions. They were logical mistakes that looked, at first, like sensible safety behavior. An early recovery might touch the port face, see resistance, unload, and try again. But if the connector had already entered the opening, an unnecessary retreat could undo most of the useful motion. In several hardware traces the controller got deep into an SC insertion and then backed out after a small regression. That was painful to watch, because the robot had already solved the part I thought was difficult.

The better description of the strategy is an axial-progress-supervised compliant state machine with force-informed recovery. Measured depth tells you what stage of insertion you are in. Force and torque are useful directional evidence, especially when they suggest a side wall or a slight tilt, but a force spike alone does not prove failure. If a connector is captured, preserving that progress matters.

An SFP diagnostic drove the same lesson home in another way. One hardware trace recorded hundreds of stall observations while the controller kept retrying an action that was no longer helping. The logs looked busy; the robot looked busy. In reality, it was running in place. More force was not the answer. A better state machine needed to preserve the evidence of failure and move on to a genuinely different recovery before the deadline was almost gone.

On real hardware, the contact controller and robot mechanics worked together. My intuition for the Cartesian compliance was a rubber band attached to a target point slightly inside the port: ask the robot to move toward that point, let the controller yield when physical contact resists it, then update the target carefully rather than forcing a rigid geometric line through the hardware.

For SC, good visual alignment often meant I could start with a gentle, largely straight descent and let the physical housing guide the last fraction of a millimeter. A small shimmy was reserved for stalls, rather than sweeping broadly across a face that we already knew how to approach. For SFP, the asymmetric entrance made a targeted lateral search more important.

This is where the opening scene comes back. The SC connector could produce two separate clicks. Earlier hardware runs brought the plug almost all the way home, but the mechanism was not fully seated. Someone familiar with the hardware pointed out the second click. With that detail, I could revisit the terminal travel and force demand and distinguish a connector that looked right from one that was actually latched.

It sounds obvious after the fact. At the time, I had never handled either type of connector in person. There are some things no amount of beautiful simulation can teach you as quickly as someone saying, “Push it a little farther. There's another click.”

06 / REAL HARDWARE

The real cable was easier. The real stakes were not.

Phase 2 moved the work to a physical robot. I had far fewer hardware sessions than I wanted, on the order of seven or eight by my recollection, and every one mattered. My process for making changes stayed familiar: observe a result, form a hypothesis, discuss it with Codex, let the development harness prepare and validate an implementation, then test the next iteration. I was still the person responsible for deciding whether an outcome actually meant what we thought it meant.

Some things simplified. In simulation I had built special movement behaviors to get around awkward cable dynamics and motion quirks. With the real cable, bending and settling were often more predictable. I could remove several workarounds and lean more on established robot motion planning. I kept fast motion for safe, open-space viewpoints and slowed the robot substantially around the board, while carrying a connector, and during final approach. Keeping the cable elevated and avoiding abrupt acceleration mattered more than shaving seconds off an individual move.

Other things became more demanding. The camera calibration mattered enormously, because my solution depended heavily on high-quality pose estimates. A small shift at the perception layer could change the entire contact behavior downstream. The choice to simplify insertion had moved effort upstream, not eliminated it.

I could reuse the same idea behind the scan laboratory: benchmark useful viewpoints on the actual setup, register the board once, keep a consistent model of the rails and ports, and use shorter targeted observations around the currently selected connector. Nothing in the robot's contact policy needed hidden simulator ground truth.

There was also a quiet benefit to having developed the whole project through a reproducible engineering workflow. I was not relying on a fragile sequence of manual UI changes that only existed in the browser. I could inspect what had changed, keep known-good versions, and work on logic even when live testing was unavailable. That did not fix the physics, but it made the engineering much less fragile.

By the penultimate sessions, the robot was bringing connectors so close to their destinations that the last step was primarily about controlled seating. Then came the lesson about the SC latch, the final tuning, and the ten-for-ten test.

07 / THE PART THAT MATTERS

I managed the system. The system managed the project.

I thought that final test gave me a chance to win. I finished eighth instead. Maybe the scored configuration exposed something I had not tested well enough. Maybe a calibration, configuration, or final-process detail was different. Maybe I made a mistake. Without decisive evidence, choosing the most satisfying explanation would just be fiction.

What is not fiction is everything I learned getting there.

After the challenge, I discussed the experience in an interview. The interviewer made a comparison I keep coming back to: an artist working in a studio. You can hire an artist to produce one painting. But the studio is what makes repeated work possible. It has the tools, the materials, the accumulated techniques, the ways to test an idea, and the people who know which part of the process comes next.

That was the thing I had built. Not just a robot policy, but the studio around it.

Codex could write code, investigate failures, assemble experiments, compare logs, and prepare the next iteration. Other agents could work on narrower questions with their own contexts. My role was to organize the studio: decide what mattered, give the agents the right tools, keep evidence straight, and refuse to confuse a successful build with a successful robot. I was limited less by the number of people I could hire than by the experiments I could afford to run and the quality of the system that coordinated them.

There is an important boundary to that analogy. Spinning up ten agents does not magically give you ten expert roboticists. Without good context, validation and clear responsibilities, it can give you ten confident ways to be wrong. And physical robots still impose limits: sensor calibration, scarce hardware time, uncertainty at contact, and a world that does not care how convincing your explanation sounds.

The robotics lesson was equally useful. Some other teams were working on extraordinarily sophisticated learned policies, and I sometimes felt like an impostor with my relatively small contact controller. But that controller was allowed to be small because I had put so much engineering around it. Better viewpoints, geometric constraints, consistent world state, versioned experiments and careful failure recovery had already removed much of its uncertainty. Make the environment intelligible, and the last action can become simpler.

I came into the challenge wondering whether someone who had been away from robotics for years, with no lab and a slow laptop, could contribute anything meaningful. I left with a top-ten finish, a final hardware session I will remember for a long time, and a reusable way of attacking difficult engineering problems.

The story is not that $20 makes someone one of the world's best roboticists. It is that the right AI engineering system changed what I could realistically attempt on my own. The robot still had to feel the edge, find the opening, and earn that second click. I just learned how to build the studio that could keep working on the problem until it did.

I couldn't afford to iterate faster, so I built a system that could iterate without me.
I managed the system. The system managed the project.
